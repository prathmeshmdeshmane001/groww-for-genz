1. The charges eval — it worked, and then it didn't

The fix from interview 1 held. The result is more interesting than a pass

Before

His form answer was ₹5. Asked to confirm it out loud:

Prathamesh: "To tere hisaab se paanch rupaye katne chahiye… wo saari milakar paanch rupaye?"

Pulin: "Nahi… ek percent at max."

Prathamesh: "That was your guess." — "Yes, one percent at max."

15:35 – 15:48

One percent of ₹500 is ₹5. The real answer is ₹18.22, or 3.6%. Wrong by about 3.6×, same direction as everyone else — but note he reached for a percentage, not a rupee figure. Same wrong model as Prakhar: charges scale with the money, so a flat fee is invisible.

Method fix from interview 1 — confirmed working

After: he answered before being told

Prathamesh: "Now do you understand how much it will be? Do you want to change your answer to just five?"

Pulin: "Kitna aa raha hai — forty something na? …Chaalees na."

29:34 – 29:51

Asked, then silence, then his number. That's the clean pre/post interview 1 failed to produce. This is the measurement the evals doc is built on.

One honest caveat: ₹40 is the figure the app showed for ₹5,000 in a stock, and the question was about ₹500. He recalled what he saw rather than re-deriving it for the smaller amount. So he learned the order of magnitude, not the rate. For interview 3: ask the post-test on the same amount as the pre-test.

And then the lesson failed

Straight after getting the number right, he drew the opposite conclusion to the one the screen was built to produce:

"But this forty is unavoidable, right?"

"Kyunki platform to kuch charge nahi kar raha hai — saare bigger players hi hain jo charge kar rahe hain… ye waali cheezein to unavoidable hain na. But is ke oopar agar mera platform bhi charge lagaata, to us ki behan ki chop bhai."

29:55 – 30:21

He is half right — STT, stamp duty and the DP charge really are third-party, not the broker's margin. But the point of the screen is that the flat ₹15.93 is what makes ₹500 in a single stock a bad idea, and that landed as "it's unavoidable, so never mind." He absolved the platform and kept the behaviour.

The eval finding that matters for the write-up: the number moved, the decision didn't. A pre/post on the charges figure is a knowledge test. It is not the same as a behaviour test, and this interview shows the two coming apart in one person, thirty seconds apart. Say that plainly in the evals doc — it's a sharper result than a clean pass would have been.

2. He found a real bug

And diagnosed it faster than we did

"Ek to tune usmein paanch hazaar daale for five years — wo per month waala scene? To comparison hi fuddu hai pehli baat."

26:02, on the active-fund tab

He is right and it is our mistake. The user picks ₹5,000 once. The active-fund tab silently switches to ₹5,000 a month for 120 months — ₹6,00,000 — and compares that. Different amount, different frequency, different question. Then:

"Yes, okay, so I've entered six lakh, six lakh. At point two five a year, what does this mean?"

26:19

And the interviewer's own note on the tape: "Theek hai, agar itni der lag rahi samajhne mein to…" (26:33).

Two of two participants have now failed on this screen. Prakhar: "wo tab confuse kiya tha jab mere ko wo comparison kara raha tha." Pulin named the cause. Fix: run the fee comparison on the amount and frequency the user actually chose, or label the switch loudly.

3. Jargon, not volume

A sharper version of Prakhar's complaint

Reading the cost ledger aloud, he hit a term and guessed wrong:

"ten rupees de diye, security transfer theek hai, kuch hota hoga — DP charge on selling… DP kya hota hai? Direct plan — stamp duty theek hai."

27:11

DP is Depository Participant. He guessed "Direct plan". The single most important line item on the screen — the flat fee the whole argument rests on — is labelled with an initialism he couldn't decode. That is very likely part of why the lesson in §1 failed.

"Seedha jargon mere munh pe na fenke. Ye main sirf ek beginner ke liye keh raha hoon, jisko kuch bhi nahi pata, jisko index fund hi nahi pata — usko kya samajh mein aayega? To itna jargon na fenke. Like zaroori hai of course, but kya koi way ho jo itna jargon na fenke aur mere se wo information nikalwa le jo actually zaroori hai aage jaane ke liye?"

33:03

Prakhar said there was too much text. Pulin says the volume isn't the problem — the undefined terms are. That's a better brief: cut initialisms and name things in words, rather than just cutting words. It also makes the case for the tap-anything tutor screen, which is still unbuilt.

4. The app asks too few questions

This contradicts the core design, and it needs handling carefully

"He's leading himself and not letting me lead."

"And this is something I am saying because not enough questions — kyunki aisi cheez hai ye jismein tum ek question mein answer ya phir kuch direction nahi le sakte. Tumhein thodi cheezein jaanni padengi. Aur kyunki ye aisa health tracker app thodi hai bhai."

"Samajh raha hai, mere paison ka khilwaad ho raha hai. To mujhe agar saamne se log question poochenge aur jaanenge, to main zyada secure feel karunga in that app — ki wo mujhe jaanna chaah raha hai, kyunki wo mere paise laga raha hai."

"Haan to it's too shallow."

30:51 – 31:36

"Main keh raha hoon ki tere app ko dekhne ke baad laga ki this is so incomplete — ye ye hona chahiye tha."

33:53

The product's central bet is that three questions is the right number, because the decision is what blocks people. Pulin says three questions is so few that it reads as the app not caring where his money goes. More questions would have increased his trust, not his friction.

That's a defensible answer and a real limit. Say both in the write-up: the design held for the user it targets, and the first out-of-ICP user rejected it on exactly the axis the design traded away.

5. He rejected the recommendation on reasoning, not numbers

And the new returns table is implicated

"Theek hai, Nifty Fifty ka humein teen saal mein return itna aa raha hai, paanch saal mein itna, das saal mein itna, large cap ka itna aa raha hai, large cap ka itna aa raha hai."

…"Maza nahi aaya mujhe."

"It showed me numbers better but I didn't like the reasoning. While I know that large caps are also not too risky. And usually large cap ke AMC ka yahi goal hota hai ki wo ek index fund ko beat kare."

23:01 – 24:09

He read the returns table added today, out loud, line by line. It worked as a thing to engage with. But it made him more confident the index fund was wrong for him, because the large-cap numbers next to it are higher.

That is exactly the risk flagged when the returns table was being argued about, and it showed up on the first user who saw it. Two honest readings, and the write-up should pick one:

  1. It's working as intended. He is an informed user who saw the full picture and made a different choice. An app that only persuades people by hiding the counter-evidence isn't the honest one. Informed opt-out is a success, not a failure.
  2. The argument is too weak to carry the table. The "0.25% is certain, their returns are not" line is on the screen but he didn't engage with it at all — he went straight to the numbers.

Both are true. The fix that serves both: put the certainty argument next to the table rather than below the fold, so the counter-evidence and the answer to it arrive together.

Two out of two, unprompted, in the closing minutes

"Ismein na phir reliability waala factor aa jaata hai — ki ultimately mere paise ke saath khela ja raha hai na. To tum jab tak mujhe assurity nahi doge tab tak main kyun karunga."

"Usse achha — jaise market khelta hai, jin factors pe khelta hai, wo agar mujhe koi bataaye taaki main khud dekh paun. Kyunki main apne aap se zyada kisko trust karunga? Kisi ko bhi nahi."

Jaise maan le har din notification aa raha hai ki ye aaj padh lo, ten minute read hai… and let's say within one month I am actually able to do things on my own. I would be very grateful for that."

35:20 – 36:12, the last substantive thing he said

Prakhar, at the same point in his interview: "I don't know which is the source of truth… main sirf tere AI pe reliable nahi hona chahiye. Mere ko baaki jagah se bhi information milni chahiye."

This is now the strongest cross-interview signal in the study. Two very different users — one who has never invested, one who invests on two platforms daily — independently ended by asking for the same thing: don't just tell me what to buy, teach me enough to check you.

Pulin adds the mechanism Prakhar didn't: a daily ten-minute read, not a course. He has already tried and abandoned Zerodha Varsity for being too big — "hafte mahine ka investment hai" (13:05). The unmet need isn't content, it's content sized to a commute.

This belongs in the 1-pager. It is the one thing both participants asked for and no incumbent ships.

8. Would he pay? No.

"Would you be willing to pay hundred rupees a month?" — "Nahi."

Why? — "Wo app mera haath pakad ke thodi le ke ja raha hai kahin. Like, is he helping me every decision? Aur agar help kar raha hai, to wo kitna reliable hai — mujhe kuch nahi pata. To nahi."

34:03 – 34:23

Prakhar said yes, Pulin says no. n=2, split 1–1, and both answered on the same axis: how much the app actually holds your hand, not price. Neither answer is evidence about pricing; together they say willingness to pay tracks perceived depth of help.

He'd pay if returns were guaranteed (34:31) — a condition that cannot exist, so discard it. And the question was leading again: "would you pay ₹100" instead of "what would you pay". Still not fixed from interview 1.

9. For interview 3

  1. Keep the silence. It produced the clean pre/post. Do not change it.
  2. Ask the post-test on the same amount as the pre-test. Pulin answered ₹40 — the ₹5,000 number — to a ₹500 question. Say "₹500 again" explicitly.
  3. Add a behaviour question after the number: "so what would you do differently?" That is where Pulin's learning broke, and we only caught it because he volunteered it.
  4. "What would you pay?" not "would you pay ₹100". Third time asking for this.
  5. Recruit in-ICP if possible — someone with a Groww account who has never invested. Two of two so far are Zerodha-led or lapsed, and Pulin is fully activated.
  6. Screen share worked much better than handing over the phone. He narrated continuously and read the screen aloud, which is why this transcript is richer. Keep it.

10. Limits

  • n = 2, both friends of the interviewer, both male, both early-career. Friendly bias throughout.
  • Pulin is out of the ICP — already investing, checks daily. His criticism is real but it is criticism from outside the target user.
  • The post-test amount was mismatched (§1), so the ₹5 → ₹40 movement is directionally right but not a like-for-like number.
  • ASR at 87.8%. It mangles names and splits sentences. Quotes above were taken where the meaning is unambiguous; the worst spots are marked [sic] in the transcript.
  • The first ~3 minutes are unrelated banter and some crude language; it is left in the transcript unedited because the instruction was to change script only.
  • He saw the updated app (returns table, horizon-aware costs, rewritten cost line). Prakhar saw the older build. The two interviews are therefore not testing the same artifact — worth stating in the evals doc.

The paragraph for the write-up

Pulin already invests. He runs a ₹5,000 recurring deposit, holds a mutual fund and gold ETFs on Zerodha, and checks his portfolio daily because he enjoys it. Kisi ko bhi nahi." That is the second participant in a row to end on the same request.
