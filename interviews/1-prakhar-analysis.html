3. Testing the app

What broke, what held

3a. The cost screen — the app's core claim — failed on wording

This is the most important product finding in the interview. Looking at the cost screen, he read the ₹43 charge as money coming to him:

"Iska matlab ye hai ki mereko — main ek saal baad bechunga to mereko forty three milenge, aur main agle hafte bechunga to mereko one point one two milenge."

Prakhar · 18:21, reading the cost screen out loud

He confirmed it later, unprompted, when asked what confused him:

"Thoda sa wordings mein mere ko samjha nahi tha. Haan, samajh gaya. Ki forty three Groww wo kaat raha hai mere — ki forty three ke mere ko mil raha hai."

Prakhar · 27:04

The whole product rests on "show the cost before the trade, in rupees, on their amount." On the first real user, the screen showed the number and he read it backwards. The maths was right; the sentence was not. Fix: lead with a verb and a minus sign — "You pay ₹43" / "−₹43 goes to charges" — not a bare figure in a table.

4. The need we had not written down

Asked at the end what we had missed, this is what he reached for

"I don't know which is the source of truth… which is the good source of information."

"Agar tera investing app hai aur mujhe invest karna hai, to main sirf tere AI pe reliable nahi hona chahiye. Mere ko baaki jagah se bhi information milni chahiye… main chahta hoon ki mere ko ye sources pata chale, taaki main wahaan se refer karke — with aur tere AI ke help se — achha, invest karna hai to ye mujhe koi bata de."

Prakhar · 32:18 – 33:14, the last thing he said in the interview

He is not asking for less guidance. He is asking for guidance he can check. He wants the app to point him outward — he named newspapers and SEBI's own site — so the recommendation stops being "because the AI said so."

Product implication: the tap-anything tutor screen (§5 of the project notes, still unbuilt) should carry sources, not only plain-language definitions. A cited explainer answers a need a real user stated out loud; an uncited one does not. He also asked for a plain SIP/returns calculator, and knew SEBI's website has one.

5. Would he pay ₹100 a month?

"Haan, kyunki main already subscriptions hain mere paas — jaise main music ko sixty rupees de raha hoon… Financial information mil rahi hai, mere paise achhi jagah invest ho rahe hain, to main kyun na karun — ninety nine per month."

Prakhar · 28:31 – 28:52
Do not over-read this

Three reasons this does not overturn the "guidance has to be free" conclusion:

  1. The question was leading. "Would you pay ₹100?" invites a yes. "What would you pay?" does not. Fix in the script.
  2. Stated, not paid. One friend, hypothetical, immediately after a good experience.
  3. The maths does not move. ₹100/month on his ₹5,000 is 24% a year. The ET Money Genius argument is arithmetic, and his willingness does not change the arithmetic. What it does show is that the perceived value of guidance at this stage is real — which is the case for an incumbent giving it away, not for charging for it.

Useful follow-up he gave for free: asked what happens if the app's pick loses money, he said he would start checking the history himself — "tab main dimaag lagaana chaalu kiya." He does not trust it blindly, which is the right instinct and supports §4.

7. What changes

In the app, in priority order

  1. Rewrite the cost line. "You pay ₹43" with the money moving away from the user, not a bare number in a table. This is the one that broke.
  2. Cut the text around every number. One point per block. His words: ekdam to the point.
  3. Fix the comparison view — it did not render side by side, and it is the screen that turns a recommendation into a checked recommendation. Keep it, simplify it.
  4. One line on SIPs: you can pause or stop any month. Removes his only stated reason for skipping mutual funds.
  5. Build the tutor screen with sources. Cited, so it answers §4 rather than adding another voice to trust.

In the interview script, before interviews 2 and 3

  1. Ask the charges question, then say nothing. Write the number down before revealing the real one. The pre/post is the strongest eval we have and this one was leaked.
  2. Read the app's own timer out loud on the done screen, so the product time is on tape, separate from the interview time.
  3. Ask "what would you pay?" not "would you pay ₹100?"
  4. Talk less. 350 interviewer turns against 308 participant turns on a 33-minute tape. Many of his answers were talked over mid-sentence.

8. Limits of this interview

  • n = 1, and he is a friend of the interviewer. Friendly bias runs through the whole tape, particularly the pricing answer and the "ye perfect lage mujhe" reaction to the recommendation.
  • The post-test on charges is void (§1a). Only the baseline stands.
  • 453 seconds is not a task time (§1b).
  • The transcript is machine-made at 84.4% confidence. It mishears "Groww" as "Crome", "Claude" as "Lord", drops the odd nahi, and splits sentences across speakers. Quotes above were picked where the meaning is unambiguous; the marked spots are flagged [sic] in the transcript.
  • He is 21, which keeps the young skew the survey already has against the brief's 20–26 band.
  • The recording stops mid-sentence at 33:18.9. If anything was said after that, it is not in this file.

The one paragraph for the write-up

Prakhar, 21, chose Groww because he had heard its AI tells you what to invest in. He finished KYC, waited for his demat account, got distracted, and two weeks later has still never invested a rupee. He guessed a ₹500 round trip would cost under ₹5 — and thought charges are a cut of your gains, so a flat fee on a flat month was invisible to him. Given a product picked by time horizon instead of by category, he invested in seven and a half minutes, sat through a ₹210 paper loss without flinching because he had already been asked when he needs the money back, and chose to do nothing. Then he asked for the thing no broker gives him: somewhere outside the app to check the app against.
